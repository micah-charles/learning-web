# Question 1 — The Black Death and Walsham

## Question

**“The Black Death divided the village of Walsham more than it united it.” How far do you agree?**

![Six-panel comic showing how the Black Death both united and divided Walsham.](/assets/history/y7/section-c/walsham_black_death_divided_united_comic.png)

*Visual argument: Christianity and fear created moments of unity, but fear, wealth inequality and labour conflict divided Walsham more deeply.*

## Core judgement sentence

**The Black Death created moments of unity through prayer and shared fear, but it divided Walsham more deeply through fleeing, unequal access to help, and conflict over wages after the plague.**

## Suggested answer

It can be argued that the Black Death divided the village of Walsham more than it united it because people reacted differently when they were afraid. Some villagers tried to protect themselves, while others were left vulnerable. For example, Thomas Bec fled when the disease approached, showing that fear could make people abandon their community. Wealthier villagers could also afford more religious protection, such as Catherine Pynfoul arranging money for a private priest, while poorer villagers had fewer choices. This shows division because the plague exposed differences in wealth, courage and access to religious support.

On the other hand, it can be argued that the Black Death also united Walsham because shared fear encouraged some people to act together. Before the plague fully arrived, villagers turned to Christianity for comfort, praying and hoping for protection. Some villagers joined together in religious actions such as pilgrimage, and others tried to protect the village from infection. Priests and helpers who gave last rites also showed a sense of duty to the community. This shows unity because the crisis encouraged people to rely on shared beliefs and collective action.

It is more convincing to argue that the Black Death divided Walsham more than it united it, although both happened. At first, fear and religion could bring people together through prayer, pilgrimage and attempts to protect the village. However, once the plague arrived and after it left, social divisions became clearer. After the plague, surviving serfs demanded higher wages, while landlords wanted to keep control through laws such as the Statute of Labourers in 1351. Overall, the Black Death created moments of unity, but its longer-term effect was to divide Walsham by fear, inequality and conflict over labour.

## Evidence to remember

- Margery Wodebite organised religious action and pilgrimage.
- Thomas Bec fled.
- Catherine Pynfoul could pay for private religious help.
- Some villagers wanted last rites before death.
- After the plague, workers wanted higher wages.
- The Statute of Labourers in 1351 tried to keep wages low.

## Quick self-test

1. What is one way the Black Death united Walsham?
2. What is one way the Black Death divided Walsham?
3. Why does the Statute of Labourers support the idea of division?
4. What is the best final judgement for this question?

# Question 2 — Athelstan and Military Conquest

## Question

**“Military conquest was the main reason Athelstan became the first king of England.” How far do you agree?**

![Six-panel comic showing Athelstan's conquest, politics, Christianity, law and kingship.](/assets/history/y7/section-c/athelstan_military_conquest_comic.png)

*Visual argument: conquest gave Athelstan power, but law, Christianity and royal authority helped him turn conquest into recognised kingship.*

## Core judgement sentence

**Military conquest gave Athelstan power, especially after the capture of York in 927, but coronation, law, councils, titles and Christian kingship helped him turn conquest into kingship.**

## Suggested answer

It can be argued that military conquest was the main reason Athelstan became the first king of England because he gained control of York in 927. York had been an important Viking stronghold, so capturing it allowed Athelstan to extend his authority far beyond Wessex and Mercia. He also forced rulers such as Constantine of Scotland and Owain of Strathclyde to recognise his power at the Council of Eamont. Later, in 937, he defeated Olaf, Constantine and Owain at the Battle of Brunanburh. This shows that military strength was vital because Athelstan used force to defeat rivals and expand his control.

On the other hand, Athelstan’s success was not only caused by conquest. He also used politics, law, religion and royal display to strengthen his authority. His coronation at Kingston in 925 helped unite Wessex and Mercia, and his use of the title *Rex Totius Britanniae*, meaning “King of all Britain”, showed that he wanted to present himself as a ruler above other kings. He also used councils and royal charters to make other rulers attend his court and accept his authority. This shows that Athelstan did not simply conquer England; he also had to govern it and make his rule appear legitimate.

It is more convincing to argue that military conquest was the most important reason Athelstan became the first king of England, but it was not enough by itself. Without the capture of York in 927, Athelstan could not have claimed such wide authority. However, his success also depended on coronation, law, councils, coins and Christian kingship. Overall, conquest gave Athelstan power, but political control and royal authority helped him turn conquest into kingship.

## Evidence to remember

- 925: Athelstan was crowned at Kingston.
- 927: Athelstan captured York.
- 927: Council of Eamont; other rulers recognised his authority.
- Title: *Rex Totius Britanniae*.
- 937: Battle of Brunanburh.
- 939: Athelstan died; some control did not last.

## Quick self-test

1. Why was York important?
2. What does *Rex Totius Britanniae* mean?
3. Why was conquest not enough by itself?
4. What is the strongest final judgement?

# Question 3 — Islamic Golden Age: Algebra and Medicine

## Question

**“Algebra was more significant than medicine in the Islamic Golden Age.” How far do you agree?**

![Six-panel comic comparing algebra and medicine in the Islamic Golden Age.](/assets/history/y7/section-c/islamic_golden_age_algebra_medicine_comic.png)

*Visual argument: algebra had a wider long-term intellectual impact, but medicine had direct practical importance for health and treatment.*

## Core judgement sentence

**Algebra was probably more significant in the long term because it shaped mathematics, science and computing, but medicine was also highly significant because it improved diagnosis, treatment and practical human life.**

## Suggested answer

It can be argued that algebra was more significant than medicine in the Islamic Golden Age because it had a very long-lasting impact on mathematics, science and modern life. Al-Khwarizmi published *Al-Jabr* in around 820, developing methods for solving equations and using unknown quantities. This was significant because algebra became one of the foundations of later mathematics, influencing European learning and remaining important today in science, engineering, finance and computing. This shows that algebra was highly significant because its influence lasted for centuries and spread far beyond the Islamic world.

On the other hand, medicine was also extremely significant because it had a direct impact on people’s health and medical knowledge. Scholars and doctors in the Islamic world collected, preserved and developed medical ideas from Greek, Roman, Persian and Indian traditions. Medical writers such as Al-Razi helped distinguish between diseases such as smallpox and measles, while Al-Zahrawi developed surgical knowledge and instruments. This shows that medicine was significant because it improved diagnosis, treatment and medical education, and its ideas influenced later doctors in Europe.

It is more convincing to argue that algebra was more significant in terms of long-term intellectual influence, but medicine was equally important for practical human life. Algebra changed the way people thought about numbers and problem-solving, and it still affects modern subjects today. However, medicine was significant because it helped doctors understand disease and treatment more clearly. Overall, algebra may be the stronger single achievement because of its lasting global influence, but the Islamic Golden Age was important because both mathematics and medicine developed together.

## Evidence to remember

- Al-Khwarizmi published *Al-Jabr* around 820.
- Algebra used unknown quantities and equations.
- Algebra influenced later mathematics, science and computing.
- Al-Razi helped distinguish diseases such as smallpox and measles.
- Al-Zahrawi developed surgical knowledge and instruments.
- Islamic medicine built on Greek, Roman, Persian and Indian learning.

## Quick self-test

1. Who wrote *Al-Jabr*?
2. Why was algebra long-lasting?
3. Why was medicine practically significant?
4. What balanced judgement should Micah use?
